# Takeaways from The Real Cost of Following Your Academic Dream

- You should at least have some realistic understanding of what post-PhD career trajectories look like and have at least a loose plan about what you want to do after your PhD, even before you start applying for graduate school.
- PhD is about learning how to fail fast and iterating on your failures to improve your research and skills.
- Networking (e.g., conference talks, 1-on-1 coffee chats, poster sessions) is important, not because it leads to job opportunities (it often won't), but because:
  - It is an opportunity to ask for career advice, ask questions about hiring practices & decisions (people are willing to share more in-person and in private), and gain insights from different perspectives (working on your research can be a bubble).
  - It exposes you to people who might be looking for collaborators or offer job opportunities in the future.
  - Don't just hang out with your labmates in conferences! Make an intentional effort to meet new people.
- Shyness is a career-killer. Try to improve your soft skills, especially how you talk to people face-to-face.
- Becoming independent is important; however, it should not come at the cost of refusing to seek mentorship from your advisors or other experienced researchers, simply because you want to appear competent. Talk to your advisors often and ask a lot of questions.
  - Ask your advisors to connect you with their network!
- Your success in academia more often depends on other people's subjective evaluation of you (e.g., how several professors perceive you in faculty hiring interviews, who writes your letters of recommendation, and what your letter writers say about you), rather than on your objective achievements.

Andrew Akbashev also wrote this in response to one of the comments on the videos, which I think is worth considering when you are applying for PhD programs:

> The biggest step in preparation for an academic career is actually picking the RIGHT PhD advisor. I didn't mention it in this lecture because the audience was already on their PhD tracks. But the choice of an advisor is everything - if their lab let you thrive, if their group has the right culture, if the advisor mentors you (and not just gives research advice), if the advisor let you explore exciting new topics that will be interesting in 5 years to present to faculty committees, if the advisor knows how to write a very strong recommendation letter, and (finally) if the advisor is relatively well known and has a strong reputation in the community, you will greatly increase your chances of getting invited for faculty interviews and landing some offers eventually. Plus, these are all important prerequisites for your own development as a researcher. If your advisor pushes you to execute their own agenda, or publish a lot of useless papers, or doesn't care to write a strong recommendation letter, then it will profoundly affect your entire career.
